## Cardiovascular System and Hematology
Myocardium is the thick muscular middle layer of the heart wall responsible for contracting and pumping blood throughout the body.
Pericardium is the double-walled fibroserous sac that surrounds and protects the heart and the roots of the great vessels.
Endocardium is the smooth inner endothelial lining of the heart chambers and valves that minimizes friction as blood flows.
Systole is the contraction phase of the cardiac cycle during which the ventricles eject blood into the aorta and pulmonary artery.
Diastole is the relaxation phase of the cardiac cycle during which the heart chambers fill with venous blood.
Cardiac output is the volume of blood pumped by each ventricle per minute, calculated as heart rate multiplied by stroke volume.
Stroke volume is the amount of blood ejected by the left ventricle during a single systolic contraction.
Sinoatrial node is the natural pacemaker of the heart located in the right atrium that initiates electrical impulses.
Atrioventricular node is the electrical relay station between the atria and ventricles that briefly delays conduction to allow ventricular filling.
Bradycardia is an abnormally slow resting heart rate typically defined as fewer than sixty beats per minute in adults.
Tachycardia is an abnormally rapid resting heart rate typically exceeding one hundred beats per minute in adults.
Arrhythmia is an irregular or abnormal heart rhythm caused by disturbances in electrical impulse generation or conduction.
Atherosclerosis is the chronic progressive buildup of lipid plaques, fibrous tissue, and calcium within arterial walls.
Ischemia is an inadequate blood supply to an organ or tissue resulting in a shortage of oxygen and nutrients needed for cellular metabolism.
Myocardial infarction is the irreversible necrosis of heart muscle tissue caused by prolonged obstruction of a coronary artery.
Hypertension is persistently elevated systemic arterial blood pressure that increases workload on the heart and vascular walls.
Hypotension is abnormally low arterial blood pressure that can lead to inadequate perfusion of vital organs.
Aneurysm is a localized abnormal dilation or ballooning of a blood vessel wall caused by structural weakness.
Thrombosis is the formation of a blood clot inside a blood vessel that obstructs normal blood flow.
Embolism is the sudden obstruction of a blood vessel by a traveling clot, air bubble, fat globule, or foreign material.
Aorta is the largest elastic artery in the human body, originating from the left ventricle to distribute oxygenated blood systemically.
Vena cava refers to the superior and inferior large veins that return deoxygenated venous blood from the body to the right atrium.
Capillary is the smallest blood vessel with thin single-cell walls enabling exchange of oxygen, carbon dioxide, nutrients, and waste.
Hemoglobin is the iron-containing metalloprotein in red blood cells that reversibly binds and transports oxygen from the lungs to tissues.
Erythrocyte is a biconcave anucleate red blood cell specialized for oxygen transport via hemoglobin.
Leukocyte is a nucleated white blood cell of the immune system that defends the body against infection and foreign substances.
Thrombocyte is a small cytoplasmic fragment also called a platelet that initiates hemostasis and blood clot formation.
Anemia is a clinical condition characterized by reduced red blood cell mass or hemoglobin concentration leading to decreased oxygen delivery.
Hemostasis is the physiological process that stops bleeding through vasoconstriction, platelet plug formation, and coagulation.

## Respiratory System
Alveoli are tiny thin-walled air sacs at the distal ends of bronchioles where pulmonary gas exchange occurs across the respiratory membrane.
Trachea is the cartilaginous windpipe that conducts inspired air from the larynx down to the primary bronchi.
Bronchus is a major conducting airway branching from the trachea into the left and right lungs.
Bronchiole is a small airway branch lacking cartilage whose smooth muscle regulates airflow resistance to the alveoli.
Larynx is the cartilaginous voice box situated between the pharynx and trachea that houses the vocal cords and protects the lower airway.
Pharynx is the muscular throat passage shared by both the respiratory and digestive tracts behind the nasal and oral cavities.
Diaphragm is the dome-shaped skeletal muscle separating the thoracic and abdominal cavities that serves as the primary muscle of inspiration.
Pleura is the double-layered serous membrane enclosing each lung and lining the thoracic cavity to reduce friction during breathing.
Surfactant is a phospholipid lipoprotein mixture secreted by type two alveolar cells that lowers surface tension and prevents alveolar collapse.
Dyspnea is the subjective clinical sensation of difficult, labored, or uncomfortable breathing.
Hypoxia is a state of insufficient oxygen availability at the cellular or tissue level to maintain normal aerobic metabolism.
Hypoxemia is an abnormally low partial pressure or concentration of oxygen dissolved in arterial blood.
Hypercapnia is an abnormally elevated level of carbon dioxide in the arterial blood resulting from inadequate alveolar ventilation.
Pneumothorax is the presence of air in the pleural cavity causing partial or complete collapse of the affected lung.
Pulmonary edema is the abnormal accumulation of extravascular fluid in the interstitial spaces and alveoli of the lungs.
Tachypnea is abnormally rapid and shallow breathing exceeding the normal respiratory rate.
Bradypnea is abnormally slow breathing below the normal physiological respiratory rate.
Apnea is the temporary cessation of spontaneous breathing lasting several seconds or longer.
Cyanosis is a bluish discoloration of the skin and mucous membranes caused by elevated levels of deoxygenated hemoglobin.
Spirometry is a pulmonary function test measuring the volume and flow rate of air inhaled and exhaled by the lungs.

## Nervous System
Neuron is the fundamental excitable cell of the nervous system that receives, integrates, and transmits electrochemical signals.
Synapse is the specialized junction where an electrical or chemical signal passes from one neuron to another target cell.
Axon is the long slender nerve fiber projecting from a neuron cell body that conducts action potentials away toward synaptic terminals.
Dendrite is a branched cytoplasmic extension of a neuron that receives incoming synaptic signals from adjacent neurons.
Myelin sheath is the insulating lipid-rich layer wrapped around axons by Schwann cells or oligodendrocytes to accelerate nerve conduction.
Action potential is a rapid transient reversal of membrane electrical potential that propagates along an excitable axon.
Neurotransmitter is a chemical messenger released from presynaptic vesicles across the synaptic cleft to bind postsynaptic receptors.
Cerebrum is the largest division of the brain divided into two hemispheres governing conscious thought, perception, language, and voluntary movement.
Cerebellum is the posterior brain structure coordinating fine motor control, posture, equilibrium, and motor learning.
Brainstem consists of the midbrain, pons, and medulla oblongata and regulates vital autonomic functions including respiration and heart rate.
Medulla oblongata is the lowest part of the brainstem containing reflex centers for breathing, blood pressure, and swallowing.
Hypothalamus is the diencephalic control center linking the nervous and endocrine systems to regulate temperature, hunger, thirst, and circadian rhythms.
Hippocampus is a curved limbic structure within the medial temporal lobe essential for consolidating short-term memory into long-term memory.
Amygdala is an almond-shaped limbic nucleus involved in processing emotion, fear conditioning, and threat detection.
Meninges are the three protective connective tissue membranes named dura mater, arachnoid mater, and pia mater covering the brain and spinal cord.
Cerebrospinal fluid is the clear colorless liquid circulating through the ventricles and subarachnoid space to cushion and nourish the central nervous system.
Aphasia is an acquired impairment of language comprehension or expression resulting from damage to cerebral language centers.
Neuropathy is dysfunction or pathological damage affecting one or more peripheral nerves causing numbness, tingling, or muscle weakness.
Meningitis is inflammation of the meningeal membranes surrounding the brain and spinal cord, commonly caused by bacterial or viral infection.
Encephalitis is acute inflammation of the brain parenchyma typically resulting from a viral infection or autoimmune response.
Stroke is an acute focal neurological deficit caused by interrupted cerebral blood flow from ischemia or intracranial hemorrhage.
Seizure is a sudden transient episode of abnormal excessive synchronous neuronal electrical activity in the cerebral cortex.

## Musculoskeletal System
Osteoblast is a bone-forming cell responsible for synthesizing and secreting organic bone matrix called osteoid.
Osteoclast is a large multinucleated cell that resorbs mineralized bone tissue during growth, remodeling, and calcium homeostasis.
Osteocyte is a mature bone cell embedded within mineralized lacunae that senses mechanical stress and maintains bone matrix.
Periosteum is the dense vascular fibrous membrane covering the outer surface of bones except at articular cartilage.
Cartilage is a flexible avascular connective tissue composed of chondrocytes in an extracellular matrix that cushions joints and shapes structures.
Tendon is a tough band of dense regular fibrous connective tissue attaching skeletal muscle to bone to transmit contractile force.
Ligament is a fibrous band of connective tissue connecting bone to bone across a joint to provide mechanical stability.
Sarcomere is the basic functional contractile unit of striated muscle extending between two adjacent Z discs.
Actin and myosin are the thin and thick contractile protein filaments that slide past one another during skeletal muscle contraction.
Atrophy is the decrease in size or wasting of a tissue, organ, or muscle due to disuse, denervation, or nutritional deficiency.
Hypertrophy is the enlargement of an organ or tissue resulting from an increase in the size of its individual constituent cells.
Osteoporosis is a systemic skeletal disorder characterized by reduced bone mineral density and microarchitectural deterioration increasing fracture risk.
Osteoarthritis is a degenerative joint disease marked by progressive breakdown of articular cartilage and subchondral bone remodeling.
Scoliosis is an abnormal lateral curvature and rotational deformity of the vertebral column.
Synovial joint is a freely movable diarthrodial joint enclosed by a capsule lined with synovial membrane secreting lubricating fluid.

## Endocrine and Metabolic Systems
Homeostasis is the dynamic physiological maintenance of a stable internal environment despite fluctuations in external conditions.
Hormone is a chemical signaling molecule secreted by an endocrine gland directly into the bloodstream to regulate distant target organs.
Pituitary gland is the master endocrine gland at the base of the brain composed of anterior and posterior lobes regulating multiple hormonal axes.
Thyroid gland is a butterfly-shaped endocrine gland in the anterior neck producing thyroxine and triiodothyronine to regulate basal metabolic rate.
Parathyroid glands are four small endocrine glands posterior to the thyroid that secrete parathyroid hormone to elevate serum calcium levels.
Adrenal cortex is the outer layer of the adrenal gland synthesizing steroid hormones including cortisol, aldosterone, and androgens.
Pancreas is a mixed exocrine and endocrine organ whose islets of Langerhans secrete insulin and glucagon to regulate blood glucose.
Insulin is a peptide hormone secreted by pancreatic beta cells that lowers blood glucose by promoting cellular uptake and glycogen synthesis.
Glucagon is a peptide hormone secreted by pancreatic alpha cells that raises blood glucose by stimulating hepatic glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis.
Hyperglycemia is an abnormally elevated concentration of glucose circulating in the bloodstream, characteristic of uncontrolled diabetes mellitus.
Hypoglycemia is an abnormally low concentration of blood glucose that can impair neurological function and cause diaphoresis or confusion.
Cortisol is the primary glucocorticoid stress hormone secreted by the adrenal cortex that increases gluconeogenesis and modulates inflammation.
Epinephrine is a catecholamine hormone and neurotransmitter released by the adrenal medulla during sympathetic fight-or-flight activation.
Negative feedback is the primary regulatory mechanism in endocrinology whereby rising hormone levels inhibit further hormone secretion.
Metabolism is the sum of all catabolic and anabolic biochemical reactions sustaining cellular energy production and biosynthesis.

## Renal and Urinary System
Nephron is the structural and functional filtration unit of the kidney consisting of a renal corpuscle and a renal tubule.
Glomerulus is a specialized tuft of fenestrated capillaries inside Bowman capsule where blood plasma is ultrafiltered under hydrostatic pressure.
Renal tubule is the segmented epithelial channel comprising the proximal convoluted tubule, loop of Henle, and distal convoluted tubule.
Glomerular filtration rate is the total volume of fluid filtered from the renal glomerular capillaries into Bowman capsule per unit time.
Reabsorption is the tubular transport process reclaiming water, glucose, amino acids, and electrolytes from filtrate back into peritubular capillaries.
Secretion is the active transport of waste products, hydrogen ions, and drugs from peritubular blood into the renal tubular lumen.
Urea is the primary nitrogenous waste product formed in the liver from protein catabolism and excreted by the kidneys in urine.
Creatinine is a breakdown product of muscle creatine phosphate filtered freely by the glomerulus and used to estimate renal function.
Diuresis is the increased production and excretion of urine by the kidneys.
Oliguria is abnormally diminished urine output, typically defined as less than four hundred milliliters per day in adults.
Anuria is the complete or near-complete absence of urine production indicating severe renal failure or urinary tract obstruction.
Hematuria is the abnormal presence of intact red blood cells in the urine, which may be microscopic or grossly visible.
Proteinuria is the presence of excessive amounts of serum protein, especially albumin, in the urine indicating glomerular injury.
Acidosis is a pathological process causing an abnormally low blood pH below seven point three five due to excess acid or base loss.
Alkalosis is a pathological process causing an abnormally high blood pH above seven point four five due to excess base or acid loss.

## Gastrointestinal and Hepatic Systems
Peristalsis is the coordinated wave-like contraction of smooth muscle that propels food and fluid along the gastrointestinal tract.
Esophagus is the muscular tube conveying swallowed food and liquid from the pharynx to the stomach through the diaphragm.
Duodenum is the first C-shaped segment of the small intestine where gastric chyme mixes with bile and pancreatic digestive enzymes.
Villi are microscopic finger-like projections of the small intestinal mucosa that vastly expand surface area for nutrient absorption.
Hepatocyte is the primary parenchymal epithelial cell of the liver performing detoxification, protein synthesis, and bile production.
Bile is a greenish-yellow alkaline fluid synthesized by hepatocytes and stored in the gallbladder to emulsify dietary fats.
Bilirubin is a yellow tetrapyrrole pigment produced from the normal breakdown of heme from senescent red blood cells.
Jaundice is the yellowish discoloration of the sclera, skin, and mucous membranes resulting from elevated serum bilirubin levels.
Cirrhosis is advanced irreversible scarring and nodular regeneration of liver parenchyma that impairs hepatic function and portal blood flow.
Hepatitis is inflammation of the liver parenchyma caused by hepatotropic viruses, toxins, alcohol, or autoimmune mechanisms.
Gastritis is inflammation of the gastric mucosal lining often associated with Helicobacter pylori infection or nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs.
Dysphagia is clinical difficulty or discomfort during the act of swallowing solids or liquids.
Ascites is the pathological accumulation of free serous fluid within the peritoneal cavity, frequently secondary to portal hypertension.

## Immunology, Pathology, and Microbiology
Antigen is any foreign or self molecular structure capable of being recognized by antibodies or lymphocyte receptors to provoke an immune response.
Antibody is a Y-shaped immunoglobulin glycoprotein secreted by plasma cells that specifically binds and neutralizes target antigens.
Lymphocyte is a mononuclear white blood cell encompassing B cells, T cells, and natural killer cells essential for adaptive immunity.
Macrophage is a large phagocytic tissue leukocyte derived from circulating monocytes that engulfs pathogens and presents antigens.
Neutrophil is the most abundant circulating granulocyte that rapidly migrates to sites of acute bacterial infection to perform phagocytosis.
Cytokine is a small secreted signaling protein mediating communication between immune cells during inflammation and host defense.
Inflammation is the protective vascular and cellular tissue response to injury or infection characterized by redness, heat, swelling, and pain.
Phagocytosis is the cellular process by which specialized leukocytes engulf and digest microorganisms, cellular debris, and foreign particles.
Anaphylaxis is a severe rapid-onset systemic allergic reaction causing bronchospasm, vasodilation, urticaria, and potentially fatal shock.
Autoimmunity is a breakdown of self-tolerance in which the immune system erroneously attacks the body's own healthy cells and tissues.
Sepsis is life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated systemic host immune response to infection.
Necrosis is uncontrolled pathological cell death caused by severe injury, ischemia, or toxins that triggers local tissue inflammation.
Apoptosis is genetically programmed orderly cell death that eliminates damaged or unneeded cells without provoking inflammation.
Edema is the abnormal accumulation of interstitial fluid within body tissues causing visible or palpable swelling.
Erythema is superficial reddening of the skin or mucous membranes caused by dilation and congestion of dermal capillaries.

## Clinical Medicine, Pharmacology, and Diagnostic Terms
Diagnosis is the systematic identification of the nature and cause of a disease through clinical history, physical examination, and diagnostic testing.
Prognosis is the medical forecast of the likely course, duration, and expected outcome of a patient's disease.
Etiology is the underlying cause or set of causal factors responsible for the origin of a medical disorder.
Pathogenesis is the stepwise biological mechanism by which an etiological agent produces structural and functional disease changes.
Prophylaxis is a preventive medical intervention, medication, or measure taken to guard against the development of disease.
Palliative care is specialized medical care focused on relieving symptoms, pain, and stress of serious illness to improve quality of life.
Acute describes a disease or symptom with rapid onset, severe intensity, and relatively short clinical duration.
Chronic describes a medical condition that develops gradually and persists over a prolonged period of months or years.
Benign refers to a non-cancerous neoplasm that grows locally without invading adjacent tissues or spreading to distant sites.
Malignant refers to a cancerous neoplasm capable of local tissue invasion and distant spread through blood or lymphatic vessels.
Metastasis is the spread of malignant tumor cells from a primary anatomical site to secondary distant organs.
Biopsy is the surgical or needle removal of a small sample of living tissue for microscopic histological examination.
Auscultation is the clinical examination technique of listening to internal body sounds of the heart, lungs, and abdomen using a stethoscope.
Palpation is the physical examination method of using the hands and fingers to assess tissue texture, tenderness, masses, and organ borders.
Percussion is the diagnostic technique of tapping the body surface to evaluate the density and resonance of underlying organs.
Idiopathic describes a medical condition or disease arising spontaneously from an unknown or obscure primary cause.
Iatrogenic describes an unintended adverse condition or complication induced inadvertently by medical treatment or diagnostic procedure.
Nosocomial refers to a hospital-acquired infection that develops during medical care and was not present at the time of admission.
Contraindication is a specific clinical factor or condition that renders a particular drug or procedure inadvisable or unsafe.
Pharmacokinetics is the study of how the body absorbs, distributes, metabolizes, and excretes a drug over time.
Pharmacodynamics is the study of the biochemical and physiological effects of drugs and their mechanisms of action on target receptors.
Bioavailability is the fraction of an administered dose of unchanged drug that reaches the systemic arterial circulation.
Half-life is the time required for the plasma concentration of a drug in the body to decrease by fifty percent.
Therapeutic index is the quantitative ratio between the toxic dose and the effective therapeutic dose indicating a drug's margin of safety.

## Medical Prefixes, Suffixes, and Word Roots
The prefix brady means slow, as seen in bradycardia for slow heart rate and bradypnea for slow breathing.
The prefix tachy means fast or rapid, as seen in tachycardia for rapid heart rate and tachypnea for rapid breathing.
The prefix hyper means excessive or above normal, as seen in hypertension and hyperglycemia.
The prefix hypo means deficient or below normal, as seen in hypotension, hypoxia, and hypoglycemia.
The prefix dys means difficult, painful, or abnormal, as seen in dyspnea for labored breathing and dysphagia for difficulty swallowing.
The prefix poly means many or excessive, as seen in polyuria for excessive urination and polydipsia for excessive thirst.
The prefix oligo means scanty or few, as seen in oliguria for diminished urine output.
The suffix itis denotes inflammation, as seen in gastritis, hepatitis, meningitis, and pericarditis.
The suffix ectomy denotes surgical excision or removal, as seen in appendectomy and thyroidectomy.
The suffix otomy denotes a surgical incision into an organ or tissue, as seen in tracheotomy and craniotomy.
The suffix ostomy denotes creating a permanent or temporary surgical opening, as seen in colostomy and tracheostomy.
The suffix pathy denotes a disease process or disorder, as seen in neuropathy, cardiomyopathy, and nephropathy.
The suffix megaly denotes abnormal enlargement of an organ, as seen in hepatomegaly and cardiomegaly.
The suffix penia denotes a deficiency or reduction in cellular count, as seen in leukopenia and thrombocytopenia.
The suffix scopy denotes visual endoscopic examination of an internal cavity, as seen in bronchoscopy and colonoscopy.
The suffix algia denotes localized pain, as seen in myalgia for muscle pain and neuralgia for nerve pain.
The suffix stenosis denotes abnormal narrowing of a bodily passage, valve, or blood vessel, as seen in aortic stenosis.
The suffix sclerosis denotes abnormal hardening or stiffening of tissue, as seen in atherosclerosis and arteriosclerosis.
The root cardio refers to the heart, neuro refers to nerves, hepato refers to the liver, and nephro refers to the kidney.
The root pulmo refers to the lungs, hemo refers to blood, osteo refers to bone, myo refers to muscle, and gastro refers to the stomach.
